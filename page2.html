How the drivers learn. Each lap is split into 20 timing sectors. In practice (and in Ghost mode) each lap a driver changes 4 of them: it moves its line sideways or changes how much grip it trusts there. A change stays only if that sector and the next one get faster without leaving the track and without traffic, pit stops or contact spoiling the lap. Radio coaching picks which corner a driver experiments on, and can teach skills it doesn't have yet.

What's modelled. The circuits are measured: the OpenStreetMap centreline is corrected using the white edge lines in aerial photos (Wallonia spring 2026, Lombardy 2018–19), which also give the width at every point. Elevation and camber at Spa come from Wallonia's 2021–22 LiDAR survey at 50 cm; Monza's elevation comes from Lombardy's 5 m terrain model, and its camber isn't measured, so it's flat. Grip is μ·(g·cos θ + v²·κᵥ + downforce): gradients push the car along the slope, crests unload the tyres and compressions load them, camber helps or hurts in the turn. Cars follow 2026 rules: F1's 768 kg cars with a ~400 kW engine and a 350 kW battery motor that fades above 290 km/h, up to 8.5 MJ deployed and 9 MJ recovered per lap, plus straight-line active aero; WEC cars with a stint energy limit; GT4 and TCR on fuel only. Tyres have compounds per series, warm up, overheat and wear to a cliff. Fuel burns off, lightening the car. Each class is calibrated so the Pro in the fastest real car laps near the real 2026 pole; per-car pace comes from real 2026 qualifying gaps. Pit-lane times come from real 2026 races. Not modelled: WEC multi-class traffic, safety-car bunching (a full-course yellow is used instead), tyre pressures, brake temperatures, wind, and a drying line.
